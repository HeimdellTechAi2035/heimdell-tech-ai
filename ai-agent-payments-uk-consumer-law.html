When an AI Agent Buys: UK Consumer Law and Proof of Mandate

Cancellation and refund rules are not wiped out because software placed the order. Not legal advice.

Quick answer: If the buyer is a consumer, they are still a consumer when an AI agent places the order. The Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 do not switch off cancellation rights because software sent the order. An AP2 mandate is meant to prove what checkout was authorised and what payment was authorised. It is evidence. It is not a waiver. This is general information for UK shops, not legal advice.

The customer can still be the consumer

Regulation 4 of the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 says a consumer is an individual acting for purposes which are wholly or mainly outside that individual’s trade, business, craft or profession. A trader is the other party. Regulation 5 defines a distance contract as a contract concluded between a trader and a consumer under an organised distance sales or service-provision scheme, without the trader and the consumer being physically present together, using only distance communication up to and including the moment the contract is concluded.

An agent that browses and checks out is another means of distance communication. The regulations do not say the right to cancel disappears if the consumer allowed software to send the order. Part 3, regulation 27, applies the cancellation rules to distance and off-premises contracts between a trader and a consumer, subject to the exceptions in the regulations. If you sell to consumers online in an organised way, assume you still have to read those rules. If you sell only to businesses, the consumer definition may not be met, and a different contract applies. Do not guess for a named order. Take advice.

The payment side of an agent checkout is described in what AP2 is and in how AP2 and UCP work together. This page is about the legal duties that sit beside the protocol.

What an AP2 mandate is meant to prove

The AP2 specification defines two mandates. The checkout mandate gives the merchant cryptographic proof that the shopping agent is authorised to purchase the checkout that was assembled. The merchant provides a merchant-signed JWT of the checkout, and the closed checkout mandate is bound to that document with a hash. The payment mandate gives the credential provider, the network and the merchant’s payment processor cryptographic proof that the agent is authorised to pay for that checkout. It is bound to the hash of the same checkout JWT.

The payment mandate includes the payee, the amount (currency and an integer in minor units) and the payment instrument. There is a human-present mode, where the person sees the closed checkout and approves it, and a human-not-present mode, where the person has approved constraints. The specification says the mandates can be used as evidence at the time of a dispute.

A mandate can show the items, amount, payee and instrument covered by the hash. It can help if someone says they never agreed that basket. It does not cancel a statutory right, and it is not a substitute for a cancellation policy.

Cooling-off and distance selling, at a high level

GOV.UK’s distance-selling guidance says that before an order you must provide, in a form the customer can save, information including your business name and address, a description, the price including taxes, how to pay, delivery arrangements and timing, and how the customer can cancel and when they lose that right. After the order you must provide a copy of the contract no later than delivery, and deliver within 30 days unless you agree otherwise.

Regulation 29 says the consumer may cancel a distance or off-premises contract at any time in the cancellation period without giving a reason, subject to listed liabilities such as diminished value from handling, the cost of enhanced delivery the consumer chose, and early performance of a service the consumer asked for. Regulation 30 sets the usual end of that period:

  • For a service contract, or digital content not supplied on a tangible medium, the period ends at the end of 14 days after the day the contract is entered into.
  • For a straightforward sales contract, it ends at the end of 14 days after the day the goods come into the physical possession of the consumer, or of a person the consumer named, other than the carrier.
  • If goods in one order arrive on different days, it runs from the last of them. For regular deliveries over a defined period of more than one day, it runs from the first of them.

GOV.UK’s returns guidance says that if the customer tells you within 14 days of receiving the goods that they want to cancel, you refund even when the item is not faulty. They need not give a reason. They have another 14 days to return it, and you refund within 14 days of getting it back, including standard delivery. Do not deduct fees unless the item was used or damaged beyond ordinary handling.

Regulation 31 extends the period if you do not give the consumer the required information about the right to cancel. The extension can run for 12 months, and if you provide the information during those 12 months the consumer then has 14 days from receiving it. GOV.UK states the same consequence. An agent checkout that hides the cancellation wording is not a clever shortcut. It can lengthen the right.

There are exceptions. Regulation 28 lists contracts where Part 3 does not apply, including goods made to the consumer’s specification or clearly personalised, goods that deteriorate or expire rapidly, newspapers and magazines other than subscriptions, and accommodation, transport, vehicle rental, catering or leisure services for a specific date. GOV.UK’s shorter list of items you only refund if faulty includes personalised goods, perishable items, newspapers and magazines, unwrapped software media, and sealed health or hygiene items once opened. Read the regulation before you rely on an exception. An agent ordering a dated event ticket is not the same case as an agent ordering a jumper.

Refunds, and why the product text matters

Separate from cooling-off, section 9 of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 treats a contract to supply goods as including a term that the quality is satisfactory, taking account of the description, the price and other relevant circumstances. Section 11 says goods sold by description must match the description. GOV.UK says you must offer a full refund if an item is faulty, not as described, or does not do what it is supposed to. Sale prices do not remove those rights, and a sign that says you do not give refunds does not remove them either.

This is where a signed mandate and clear product data meet. If the checkout the merchant signed said “wool jumper, blue, size M, £40”, the mandate is bound to that checkout. If the jumper that arrives is a different product, the description term and the refund guidance are about the goods, not about whether an agent clicked. If the checkout text was empty and the only clue was a picture, you have a weaker record of what was authorised and a weaker record of what “as described” meant.

Card-scheme chargebacks are a separate process from the statutory cancellation right. This page does not set card-scheme time limits or win rates, because those rules are not the Consumer Contracts Regulations and we are not citing a scheme manual. What AP2 adds, according to its own specification, is evidence of the authorised checkout and payment if a dispute is raised. Keep the mandate, the checkout document it hashes, and the product text. Do not assume the mandate ends the conversation.

Regulation 34 requires reimbursement of what the consumer paid, including standard delivery unless they chose a dearer option, without a fee, and generally by the same payment method. For goods, if you are not collecting them, the deadline is 14 days after the goods come back or, if earlier, after the consumer shows they were sent. Otherwise it is 14 days after you are told of the cancellation.

How this joins UCP

UCP is how an agent reads a catalogue and starts a checkout. AP2 is how that checkout is authorised for payment. The merchant-signed checkout, which the mandate binds to, is only as clear as the product data, the price, the delivery option and the returns wording inside it. Shops preparing that text are doing the work described on UCP readiness and in the small-business agentic commerce guide.

Put the cancellation wording where an agent can read it as text, not only in an image. Tell the customer, before the order, how to cancel and when the right ends. Give them a copy they can save. Those are the distance-selling duties above. They apply to the consumer whether a person or an agent pressed the last button.

Frequently asked questions

Does an AI agent cancel the customer's cooling-off right?

Not by itself. The Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 give a consumer a right to cancel many distance contracts during the cancellation period without giving a reason. The regulations do not contain an exception for an order placed by software. Whether a particular contract is a distance contract, and whether an exception applies, depends on the facts. This is not legal advice.

What is an AP2 mandate meant to prove?

The AP2 specification describes a checkout mandate as cryptographic proof that the shopping agent is authorised to purchase a particular checkout, and a payment mandate as proof that the agent is authorised to pay for that checkout. Both are bound to the merchant-signed checkout. The specification says they can be used as evidence in a dispute. They do not replace statutory cancellation or refund duties.

How long is the cooling-off period for goods bought at a distance?

For a sales contract, the cancellation period normally ends 14 days after the day the goods come into the consumer's physical possession. For services, and for digital content not supplied on a tangible medium, it normally ends 14 days after the day the contract is entered into. There are different rules for split deliveries, and exceptions where the right does not apply. If the trader does not give the required cancellation information, the period can be extended.

Do business buyers get the same cancellation right?

The cooling-off rules in Part 3 apply to distance and off-premises contracts between a trader and a consumer. A consumer is an individual acting wholly or mainly outside their trade, business, craft or profession. A limited company, or a sole trader buying mainly for the business, may not be a consumer for that purchase. Check the contract and take advice.
